Reliance Industries: 72-Hour Crisis Brief

Jamnagar DTA + SEZ (1.4M bpd) | Indian Basket: $156/bbl | Decision Deadline: April 3, 2026 | 9 Buckets, 107 Questions
Problem Statement

Within the next 72 hours (by April 3, before the Russia sanctions waiver expires), what should the CEO, CFO, Head of Refining, and Head of Supply at Reliance Industries (Jamnagar, 1.4 mbpd) decide regarding crude procurement source-switching, run-rate optimization, product export pricing, petrochemical feedstock locking, and balance-sheet deployment — in order to capture a margin uplift of at least $4–6/bbl above normalized GRM within the April–June 2025 quarter — given Hormuz mining that has suspended normal Gulf liftings, Indian basket at $156/bbl creating elevated crack spreads and working capital stress, MRPL’s shutdown removing a domestic competitor and creating a spot product arbitrage window, and the simultaneous expiry of the Russia waiver?

Decision Sensitivity — $142/bbl. Above $142, aggressive run maximization is correct. Below $142, switch to defensive (reduce throughput, shorten tenors, increase hedging). Secondary: if >60% of Russian volume lost post-Apr 3, run-max reverses at any price.

The single highest-return immediate action is authorizing Jamnagar’s yield optimization within 24 hours — generating $3–6M/day at zero capital cost — while executing a CEO-level call to Aramco for Arab Light via Yanbu within 12 hours. The critical risk is execution sequence failure: committing to spot crude purchases before written OFAC counsel opinion is in hand. By April 3, the Board must decide: whether RIL will execute an immediate Arab Light volume uplift, and whether the CFO will draw maximum revolving credit today.

Core Hypotheses

Five bets this analysis makes. If any flip, the recommendation reverses.

H1
The Hormuz closure is a margin opportunity for Jamnagar, not a threat. At $156/bbl crude, product crack spreads are so wide that running at maximum capacity generates outsized profits.
This reverses if the Indian basket drops below $142/bbl. Below that level, the risk of holding expensive crude inventory during a sudden ceasefire-driven price collapse outweighs the margin from selling products. The inflection is sharp: above $142, attack; below $142, defend.
H2
India buys Russian crude under an informal US policy of non-enforcement — not a formal legal waiver. That tolerance is expected to expire around April 3. If it does, and RIL loses more than 60% of its Russian supply, no product margin is wide enough to compensate for the cost of replacing those barrels.
Russian crude accounts for ~15% of Jamnagar’s intake. The replacement options (West Africa, Brazil, US Gulf) cost $8–20/bbl more per barrel and take 18–35 days to arrive. RIL must have alternative cargoes contracted before that deadline or face a forced rate cut within three weeks.
H3
The biggest risk is not the oil market — it is how fast Reliance’s own organization can move. Adjusting Jamnagar’s product yields to favor diesel and jet fuel costs nothing and is worth $3–6M per day, but it requires internal sign-off that has not happened yet.
Every decision in this brief depends on the one before it. The correct sequence starts with a legal opinion on India’s sanctions exposure, then insurance confirmation, then crude procurement, then throughput commitment. Buying spot crude before getting the legal opinion could trigger US secondary sanctions — an error that dwarfs any margin opportunity.
H4
There is a scenario nobody has modeled: crude prices stay high because of the supply shock, but product demand collapses because the price spike triggers a recession. If that happens, running Jamnagar at maximum throughput actually loses money.
This is what happened in 2008. Asian diesel crack spreads fell from $25/bbl to $8/bbl in six months as demand cratered. Below an $8/bbl gasoil crack, the incremental cost of running at full rate (freight, insurance, operational stress) exceeds the margin per barrel. The CFO must stress-test this scenario before approving any throughput increase.
H5
Jamnagar is the most complex refinery on Earth. It can process 216 different crude grades. When simpler refineries are forced to shut down because they cannot handle substitute crudes, Jamnagar keeps running — and captures their market share.
At $156/bbl, Jamnagar’s estimated refining margin is +$5–7 per barrel. Government-owned refineries with simpler configurations (like HPCL Mumbai) are losing money on every barrel they process. This gap does not close when the crisis ends — every crisis month widens Reliance’s structural advantage permanently.

Scenarios

Three scenarios for the Hormuz crisis. All procurement planning assumes Scenario B. Even Scenario A requires 21+ days of mine clearance before shipping resumes.

A. Rapid Diplomatic Resolution (≤7 days)15–20%

US-Iran backchannel produces IRGC stand-down. Mine coordinates shared. But even with Day 1 political resolution, physical mine clearance of the commercial lane takes a minimum 21–30 days. The strait does not reopen the day shooting stops.

B. Negotiated Ceasefire + Minesweeping (30–60 days)50–55% — BASE CASE

One lane partially cleared. Saudi/UAE diplomatic pressure on Iran. IRGC shares partial mine data. Historical precedent: Gulf of Suez 1984 took ~3 months for primary lanes; Iran-Iraq War 1987–88 took 4–8 months for full restoration. Partial lane in 30–60 days is achievable but requires active Iranian cooperation.

C. Extended Conflict (90+ days)30–35%

Iran refuses mine data. Active IRGC interdiction continues. Iran has 2,000–5,000 naval mines including EM-52 rocket-propelled mines that defeat conventional sweeping. Full clearance under non-cooperative scenario: 4–6 months. This scenario justifies the most aggressive procurement strategy.

Decision Gate Sequence

Five gates in strict serial order. No gate opens until the prior gate clears. Getting the sequence wrong is worse than being slow.

Gate 1 — Legal PerimeterT+6h

Written legal opinion on sanctions exposure post-April 3. Is the risk a formal OFAC waiver expiry or a US policy forbearance withdrawal? Every downstream decision is sized against this answer. Owner: General Counsel + External Sanctions Counsel.

Gate 2 — Insurance ConfirmationT+12h

Which P&I clubs and war-risk underwriters will cover cargoes through current Arabian Sea routing? Without confirmed cover, no spot purchase can be made — crude physically cannot move. Owner: CFO + Head of Shipping.

Gate 3 — Alternative Crude LockedT+18h

Confirmed cargo availability from West Africa, Brazil, Saudi Yanbu, or US Gulf. Refinery compatibility verified for each grade. 2–4 West Africa cargoes loadable within 10 days. Aramco via Yanbu is fastest (10–15 day transit). Owner: Head of Supply + Head of Refining.

Gate 4 — Throughput CommitmentT+30h

Set Jamnagar run-rate targets for the 30–45 day forward horizon based on confirmed crude volumes from Gates 2 and 3. Cannot precede Gate 3. Owner: Head of Refining, ratified by CEO.

Gate 5 — Product Export/Domestic SplitT+48h

Determine SEZ export vs. DTA domestic sale split. Execute forward product commitments. SEZ domestic diversion requires MoPNG/DGFT approval — regulatory track must start at T+0 in parallel. Owner: Head of Supply + CFO.

Parallel track (starts at T+0): CEO-level engagement with MoPNG Secretary for SEZ flexibility and emergency policy accommodation. This cannot wait for Gates 1–4.
