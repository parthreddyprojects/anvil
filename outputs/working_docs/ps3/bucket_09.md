## L1.1 — Force Majeure Provisions in Crude Purchase Contracts

**BOTTOM LINE:** FM clauses almost certainly exist in all major crude supply contracts, but whether they suspend payment (vs. merely supply obligations) without triggering termination is contract-specific language that no public source confirms. MoPNG legal counsel must produce actual contract text within 24 hours.

**DATA GAP + EXTRAPOLATED**

**What is structurally known:**
- HPCL, BPCL, CPCL, IOCL, and NRL are PSU refiners purchasing crude under term contracts (typically annual or multi-year) with Saudi Aramco (Arab Light/Heavy), ADNOC (Murban), SOMO (Basrah), and KPC (Kuwait Export). This is confirmed by PPAC import data and company ARs.
- **Confidence: HIGH | Source: PPAC FY2024 import data; company ARs 2023-24**

**EXTRAPOLATED — FM Clause Structure (reasoning chain):**
Standard crude oil term contracts (the industry norm for NOC-to-NOC or NOC-to-refiner agreements) follow Platts/ISDA-adjacent frameworks. FM clauses in these contracts **typically cover supply-side events** (force majeure on the seller's ability to deliver) rather than buyer-side payment incapacity. Key structural points:

- **Saudi Aramco term contracts:** Historically contain FM provisions triggered by events beyond the seller's control (war, natural disaster, government action). A buyer invoking FM due to *inability to pay* — as distinct from inability to lift crude — is **not standard FM territory**. FM is a supply-side instrument, not a credit instrument.
- **EXTRAPOLATED confidence: MEDIUM** — based on standard industry contract architecture; actual Aramco/ADNOC/SOMO contract language is not public.
- **Critical distinction:** The current crisis is a *logistics/sanctions/financial* crisis, not a physical force majeure. Invoking FM for non-payment would be legally aggressive and likely contested by counterparty NOCs.
- **SOMO (Iraq):** SOMO contracts historically contain relatively protective language for buyers, with dispute resolution through arbitration. Iraq's own fiscal dependence on oil revenues means SOMO would resist payment suspension.
- **KPC:** Similar structure; Kuwait has historically been a stable counterparty.

**Most protective FM scenario (EXTRAPOLATED):**
If the Hormuz mining event itself constitutes a force majeure (i.e., physical impossibility of lifting cargo through a mined strait), **seller-side FM may be invoked by Aramco/ADNOC/SOMO first**, releasing both parties from delivery/payment obligations for the duration. This is the **legally cleanest outcome** for Indian refiners — it avoids buyer-side FM invocation entirely.

- **EXTRAPOLATED confidence: MEDIUM**
- **Reasoning:** 1987-88 Gulf War tanker crisis established precedent for seller-side FM invocation during Hormuz disruption.

**MoPNG legal counsel status:** DATA GAP — no evidence in research that MoPNG has reviewed FM language across these contracts in the current scenario. **This review must be initiated within 12 hours of this briefing.**

**DECISION IMPLICATION:** Do not invoke buyer-side FM unilaterally — instead, pressure counterparty NOCs to invoke seller-side FM first, which provides cleaner legal protection; MoPNG legal counsel must produce contract FM language within 24 hours.

## L1.2 — Board Fiduciary Obligations vs. MoPNG Ministerial Direction

**BOTTOM LINE: A written ministerial directive alone is insufficient legal protection for individual directors. A formal Presidential Directive under Section 20 of the Companies Act, 1956 (or equivalent Government Company provisions) is the correct instrument and must be issued before boards are asked to authorize continued loss-making operations.**

**DATA GAP + EXTRAPOLATED**

**Statutory framework (confirmed):**
- HPCL, BPCL, CPCL (IOCL parent), and NRL are "Government Companies" under Section 2(45) of the Companies Act, 2013. The President of India, acting through MoPNG, holds majority shareholding.
- **Confidence: HIGH | Source: Companies Act, 2013; MCA filings; PPAC ownership data**
- Under Section 166 of the Companies Act, 2013, directors have a fiduciary duty to act in the best interests of the company. Sustained negative-GRM operations **prima facie expose directors to claims of breach of fiduciary duty** by minority shareholders (LIC, retail investors hold minority stakes in HPCL, BPCL, IOCL).
- **Confidence: HIGH | Source: Companies Act, 2013, Section 166**

**EXTRAPOLATED — Negative GRM threshold for fiduciary breach:**
At $156/bbl Indian basket with frozen retail prices:
- Estimated diesel under-recovery: ₹18–22/litre (**Source: Research C10, MEDIUM confidence**)
- Estimated petrol under-recovery: ₹12–16/litre (**Source: Research C10, MEDIUM confidence**)
- At these levels, **every barrel processed generates certain cash loss**. A director approving continued full-throughput operations without government indemnification is exposed.
- **EXTRAPOLATED confidence: HIGH** — the legal exposure is clear under standard corporate law; the only question is the protective instrument.

**The correct legal instrument:**
- **Section 20 of the Companies Act, 1956** (the applicable precedent — used during nationalization era) and its Companies Act 2013 equivalent allow the Central Government to issue directions to Government Companies in the public interest.
- More practically: **A Cabinet decision + Presidential Directive** (issued via MCA/MoPNG) explicitly directing continued operations in the national interest, with an undertaking of government indemnification for losses, provides **statutory cover** for individual directors.
- A ministerial letter or verbal direction is **legally insufficient** — it does not override the Companies Act fiduciary framework.
- **EXTRAPOLATED confidence: HIGH** — this is established Indian corporate law doctrine for Government Companies.

**Minority shareholder risk:**
- BPCL: Government holds ~52.98%; LIC + retail = ~47%. Minority shareholders **can file derivative suits** if directors approve sustained loss operations without government cover.
- HPCL: Government holds ~54.9% via ONGC; similar minority exposure.
- **Confidence: HIGH | Source: BSE shareholding data, Q3 FY2025**

**DECISION IMPLICATION:** MoPNG must issue a formal Cabinet-backed Presidential Directive with explicit loss indemnification language within 48 hours — a ministerial letter does not protect individual directors and exposes PSU boards to derivative litigation from minority shareholders.

**Source: Research S3A (PPAC supply data); Confidence: HIGH**

**EXTRAPOLATED — Categories of default exposure:**

1. **Defense establishments:** MoD/armed forces supply agreements are typically government-to-government; penalty clauses are unlikely but strategic consequences (operational readiness) are severe. **NRL and CPCL are critical for Northeast and Southern Command respectively.**

2. **Aviation (ATF):** DGCA-regulated; airlines (Air India, IndiGo, SpiceJet) have ATF supply agreements with OMCs. Default triggers replacement sourcing at spot prices; refiners may face **differential cost liability** (spot minus contract price × volume). EXTRAPOLATED aggregate: **$50–150M across six refineries over 30-day disruption** — LOW confidence, wide range.

3. **Fertilizer plants (naphtha):** BPCL Kochi supplies naphtha to Kerala/TN fertilizer plants. Naphtha supply default triggers urea production shortfall; contractual penalties likely; more importantly, ECA/fertilizer ministry intervention likely. **Confidence: MEDIUM**

4. **State government PDS/bitumen:** Road construction bitumen contracts (CPCL Manali is a major bitumen supplier to Tamil Nadu PWD) — penalty clauses typical at 0.5–1% of contract value per week delay. **EXTRAPOLATED LOW confidence.**

**NRL Critical Flag:** NRL Numaligarh is effectively the **sole commercial fuel supplier for much of Northeast India** (Research S3A). Any throughput reduction below minimum supply obligations here triggers not just contractual default but a humanitarian/strategic emergency with no substitution pathway within 72 hours.

**ECA Protection:**
- Essential Commodities Act, 1955 (amended 2020) allows the Central Government to declare controlled articles and direct production/distribution. A formal ECA notification specifically covering petroleum products in crisis conditions **would provide statutory force majeure protection** against private party claims for supply default.
- **Confidence: HIGH (statutory basis) | Source: ECA 1955 as cited in Research C10**
- Research C10 confirms ECA is available but **not currently invoked** as of early 2025.
- **Invocation timeline:** ECA notification can be issued by gazette notification within 24–48 hours — no parliamentary approval required for initial declaration.

**DECISION IMPLICATION:** MoPNG should issue an ECA notification covering petroleum products within 24 hours — this simultaneously provides statutory protection from supply contract default claims and creates the legal basis for directing private refiners (L1.5 below).

## L1.4 — Insurance Policy Activation

**BOTTOM LINE: Insurance notification windows are almost certainly expiring or have expired if the Hormuz mining event occurred on March 28. Notices of Circumstance must be filed immediately regardless of coverage assessment. The Hormuz mining event's characterization as "war risk" vs. "business interruption" will be the central coverage dispute.**

**DATA GAP + EXTRAPOLATED**

**No insurance policy data exists in the research corpus for any of the six refineries.** The following is entirely EXTRAPOLATED from standard industry insurance practice.

**EXTRAPOLATED — Policy types and coverage analysis:**

**1. Marine Cargo / War Risk Insurance:**
- Indian PSU refiners purchase crude under CIF or CFR terms via OMC trading arms (IOCL, BPCL, HPCL have trading subsidiaries). Marine war risk insurance covers vessels transiting conflict zones.
- The Hormuz mining event **almost certainly qualifies as a war risk peril** under Lloyd's/London Market war risk clauses (Institute War Clauses).
- **Implication:** War risk premiums have spiked (analogous to Red Sea 2024 crisis where premiums rose 10–15× for Gulf of Aden transits). This increases landed crude cost even for non-Hormuz alternatives.
- **Confidence: MEDIUM (structural inference) | Recency: Red Sea analog, 2024**

**2. Business Interruption (BI) Insurance:**
- BI coverage for refineries typically requires a **physical damage trigger** at the insured premises. A crude supply shortfall caused by external geopolitical events **without physical damage to the refinery** is **unlikely to be covered** under standard BI policy language.
- The exception: **contingent BI** or **supply chain BI** endorsements, which some large refiners purchase. Whether any of the six PSU refiners carry such endorsements is DATA GAP.
- **Confidence: MEDIUM (standard market practice)**

**3. Notification Windows:**
- Standard BI and property policies require notification of circumstances "as soon as reasonably practicable" or within specific windows (7–30 days typical). If the crisis began March 28, **April 4 may be outside the 7-day window** for some policies.
- **EXTRAPOLATED — URGENT:** Legal/insurance teams at each refinery must file Notices of Circumstance **today** (April 1) regardless of coverage uncertainty. Failure to notify prejudices future claims.
- **Confidence: HIGH (insurance law principle)**

**4. Directors & Officers (D&O) Insurance:**
- Given the fiduciary risk identified in L1.2, D&O insurers must also be notified of the circumstance of potential board liability from continued loss-making operations. This protects individual directors even if the coverage ultimately does not respond.
- **EXTRAPOLATED confidence: MEDIUM**

**DECISION IMPLICATION:** Insurance teams at all six refineries must file Notices of Circumstance to all relevant insurers today — coverage disputes can be resolved later, but failure to notify today may void valid claims entirely.

## L1.5 — Emergency Powers Over Private Refiners (Reliance, Nayara)

**BOTTOM LINE: The Government of India has substantial statutory powers to direct private refiners under the ECA 1955 and the Petroleum Act 1934, but the PNGRB Act 2006 creates a regulatory buffer. These powers are legally available within 72 hours but politically extremely sensitive given Reliance's economic weight. The more feasible path is negotiated commercial arrangement rather than compulsory direction.**

**DATA GAP + EXTRAPOLATED (partial statutory basis confirmed in research)**

**Confirmed statutory framework:**

**1. Essential Commodities Act, 1955 (ECA):**
- Research C10 confirms ECA is available and covers petroleum products. Under ECA, the Central Government can:
  - Control production, supply, distribution of essential commodities
  - Direct any producer (including private) to maintain minimum production/supply
  - Fix prices for controlled commodities
- **Criminal enforcement: up to 7 years imprisonment** for non-compliance (confirmed, Research C10)
- **Confidence: HIGH | Source: ECA 1955 as cited in Research C10**
- **Limitation (EXTRAPOLATED):** The ECA 2020 amendment **narrowed the scope** of ECA applicability for agricultural commodities but petroleum remains covered under central list. However, compelling a private refiner to operate below commercial viability using ECA has **not been tested in post-liberalization India**.

**2. Petroleum Act, 1934 + Petroleum Rules:**
- Grants the Central Government authority to regulate petroleum operations broadly. Could theoretically be used to direct refinery operations.
- **Confidence: MEDIUM (statutory basis exists; operational application extrapolated)**

**3. PNGRB Act, 2006:**
- PNGRB (Petroleum and Natural Gas Regulatory Board) regulates midstream/downstream. PNGRB has authority to direct entities to maintain supply. However, **refinery operations are upstream of PNGRB's primary jurisdiction** (which covers pipelines and retail).
- **Confidence: HIGH | Source: PNGRB Act, 2006**

**EXTRAPOLATED — Reliance/Nayara Specific Analysis:**

*Reliance Industries (Jamnagar — not one of the six, but relevant to regional product supply):*
- Reliance is India's largest private refiner (1.24 mb/d combined Jamnagar capacity). RIL supplies significant volumes into the domestic market.
- RIL has historically cooperated with government during supply crises but under commercial arrangements, not statutory compulsion.
- **Compelling RIL to share crude** with PSU refiners under ECA would be legally novel and commercially confrontational — RIL's crude procurement contracts are proprietary.
- **Political feasibility: LOW** for compulsory direction; **MEDIUM-HIGH** for negotiated commercial arrangement with government backstop.

*Nayara Energy (Vadinar, 405 kbpd):*
- Rosneft holds ~49.13% stake; Trafigura ~24.5%. **Sanctions dimension:** Directing a Rosneft-owned entity to comply with Indian government directives in a Russia-adjacent crisis has **additional geopolitical complexity**.
- Nayara's crude diet relies heavily on Russian Urals (non-Hormuz) — making it potentially **less affected** by the crisis and a candidate for increased domestic product supply rather than crude sharing.
- **Confidence: MEDIUM | Source: Nayara ownership — FT/Reuters 2022; Russian crude diet — PPAC import data inference**

**72-Hour Legal Feasibility:**
- ECA notification: **Achievable in 24 hours** (gazette notification)
- Directing specific product supplies from private refiners to deficit regions: **Achievable under ECA within 48 hours** with legal foundation
- Compelling crude sharing with PSU refiners: **Legally available but operationally and politically HIGH RISK**
- **EXTRAPOLATED confidence: MEDIUM**

**DECISION IMPLICATION:** MoPNG should use ECA to direct Nayara Energy (less politically sensitive, Russian crude diet means it is less crisis-affected) to prioritize domestic product supply to deficit regions, while pursuing Reliance cooperation through negotiated commercial arrangement rather than statutory compulsion.
