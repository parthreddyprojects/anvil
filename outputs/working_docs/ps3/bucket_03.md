## 3.7 — Restart Timeline and Cold vs. Hot Standby Asymmetry

**BOTTOM LINE: The asymmetric cost of cold shutdown vs. hot standby minimum throughput creates an unambiguous operational preference — maintain minimum stable throughput (40–50% nameplate) rather than cold shutdown at all 6 refineries.**

**Cold shutdown restart timeline:**
- Industry benchmark (Turner & Townsend 2022, cited C06): **10–15 days** CDU cold restart to stable throughput
- Additional downstream unit restarts (HDS, reformer, coker if applicable): **+5–10 days** for complex train restabilization
- **Full refinery operational restart from cold: 15–25 days.** Source: C06 (MRPL restart documentation, 2017 flood precedent cited). **Confidence: HIGH.**
- NRL-specific: Waxy Assam crude processing creates additional complexity — cold restart requires pre-heating entire crude handling system. **NRL restart may be 20–30 days.** Source: C12 (Assam crude high pour point constraints). **Confidence: MEDIUM.**

**Cost of cold shutdown (30-day period):**
- Fixed costs continue regardless: ~₹15–25 cr/day for a mid-size refinery in manning, security, utilities, catalyst maintenance. Source: Industry benchmark. **Confidence: MEDIUM (EXTRAPOLATED).**
- Recommissioning inspection and safety certification: ₹10–30 cr one-time per refinery.
- Catalyst regeneration if hydrotreaters cooled improperly: ₹20–80 cr per unit. **Confidence: MEDIUM.**

- Minimum throughput (40–50% nameplate): produces some product (covering regional obligations partially), avoids restart costs, keeps workforce skilled and engaged.

## 3.11 — Equipment Degradation Risk at 40–50% Throughput for 30+ Days

**BOTTOM LINE: Sustained 40–50% throughput for 30 days carries manageable but real equipment risk at all 6 refineries; the highest-risk units are fired heaters (tube coking risk), crude preheat trains (fouling acceleration), and hydrotreater reactors (maldistribution). HPCL Mumbai's aging infrastructure and CPCL Manali's vintage units are highest risk.**

**[EXTRAPOLATED — no unit-specific inspection data in research. Based on process engineering principles and OISD guidelines.]**

**Fired heater risk (applicable to all refineries):**
- At 40–50% throughput, tube-side velocity in crude preheat furnaces falls below design minimum. Below ~40% design flow, **film boiling and local hot spots** can develop, accelerating tube coking and creep. Risk becomes significant after **15–20 days** of continuous low-rate operation.
- OISD Guideline 160 (Fired Heaters) specifies minimum flow requirements; operators must increase recirculation to maintain tube velocity. This is a standard operational response but requires continuous monitoring.
- Source: OISD-GL-160; API 560 (Fired Heaters for General Refinery Service). **Confidence: HIGH (technical standard basis).**

**HPCL Mumbai — specific risk:**
- HPCL Mumbai's refinery includes units commissioned in the 1950s–1970s (original Mahul refinery). Older tube metallurgy (carbon steel vs. modern Cr-Mo alloy) has lower tolerance for thermal cycling at reduced flow rates.
- **Assessment:** HPCL Mumbai has the highest irreversible damage risk in the group at sustained 40% throughput. Operations team should target 50–55% minimum and increase fired heater inspection frequency to daily.
- Source: HPCL Annual Report FY2024 (capex on refinery modernization ongoing, implying legacy units still in service); industry benchmark for aging CDU infrastructure. **Confidence: MEDIUM (EXTRAPOLATED).**

**CPCL Manali — specific risk:**
- Original CDU commissioned 1965 (revamped 1990s and 2000s). Crude preheat train fouling at low throughput is a documented operational challenge for high-TAN (Total Acid Number) crude processing at Manali.
- At 40% throughput with any high-TAN alternative crude (some West African grades per C04 research), **naphthenic acid corrosion risk increases** due to reduced dilution and lower flow velocity in atmospheric column overheads.
- **Assessment:** CPCL should not process high-TAN crude grades (TAN >0.5) at below 55% throughput without metallurgical inspection clearance. Limits choice of alternative crude grades.
- Source: CPCL FY2024 Annual Report (refinery configuration); Naphthenic acid corrosion industry literature (NACE SP0403). **Confidence: MEDIUM (EXTRAPOLATED).**

**NRL Numaligarh — specific risk:**
- Waxy Assam crude (pour point ~38°C, C12 research) creates wax deposition risk in cold spots during low-throughput operation. At 40–45% throughput, crude column bottom temperature may drop below wax deposition threshold in poorly insulated sections.
- **Restart after wax-plugged lines is extremely costly** — requires steam purging and potentially physical pigging of crude lines. NRL's isolation in Northeast makes emergency maintenance response slow.
- **Assessment:** NRL's minimum safe throughput floor should be set at 50% nameplate (30 kbpd), not 40%. Operating below this for >10 days risks irreversible wax deposition in CDU feed lines.
- Source: C12 (Assam crude physical properties — high pour point confirmed HIGH confidence). **Confidence: MEDIUM (EXTRAPOLATED).**

**Hydrotreater/catalyst risk (all refineries):**
- HDS and naphtha hydrotreater units have **minimum hydrogen partial pressure requirements**. At 40–50% throughput, recycle gas compressor turndown may reduce hydrogen partial pressure below catalyst design minimum, accelerating coke deposition on catalyst.
- At 30 days of sub-optimal hydrogen partial pressure, catalyst life shortens by estimated **20–40%** (requiring earlier scheduled regeneration). This is recoverable but adds planned maintenance cost post-crisis.
- Source: HALDOR TOPSOE catalyst operating guidelines (industry standard reference); API 941 (Nelson Curves for hydrogen service). **Confidence: MEDIUM (EXTRAPOLATED).**

**DECISION IMPLICATION:** Crisis cell must require each refinery's technical director to file a 48-hour equipment risk assessment specifically for CDU fired heaters and hydrotreater hydrogen partial pressure — these are the two failure modes that could convert a managed reduced-rate operation into an unplanned emergency shutdown.

## 3.14 — Scheduled Turnarounds: Bring Forward, Defer, or Hold

**BOTTOM LINE: No confirmed April/May 2025 turnaround schedules are available in public research for this refinery group. Based on industry turnaround frequency norms, 1–2 of these refineries are statistically likely to have a turnaround window in Q1–Q2 FY2026. Deferral by 60–90 days is generally permissible with OISD concurrence; bringing forward a turnaround under crisis conditions requires careful management to avoid consuming scarce maintenance resources.**

**[DATA GAP — specific turnaround schedules for HPCL Mumbai, BPCL Mumbai, BPCL Kochi, CPCL Manali, IOCL Bina, NRL Numaligarh for April–May 2025 not found in available research. Following based on industry norms and regulatory framework.]**

**Turnaround frequency norms for Indian PSU refineries:**
- Major CDU turnaround (full unit shutdown): Every **4–5 years** per unit. Running turnaround cycle means statistically ~20–25% of refinery CDUs are due for turnaround in any given year.
- Minor turnaround (catalyst change, heat exchanger bundle, safety valve certification): Every **18–24 months.**
- Source: OISD Guideline 105 (Inspection and Safe Practices during Turnaround); Petroleum Industry Standard. **Confidence: HIGH.**

**Regulatory constraints on deferral:**
- OISD-105 permits turnaround deferral with **written concurrence from OISD regional office** and Chief Inspector of Factories (state). Statutory inspection certificates (under Factories Act 1948) require periodic renewal — deferral beyond 12 months of last inspection is not permissible without explicit waiver.
- **Practical deferral window: 60–90 days is achievable** for most units if last turnaround was more than 18 months ago and no critical equipment flag has been raised. Beyond 90 days, OISD waiver requirement becomes more onerous.
- Source: OISD-105; Factories Act 1948 (Section 7A — general safety duties); Petroleum Rules 2002. **Confidence: HIGH.**

**Bringing forward a turnaround — crisis logic:**
- If a refinery has a turnaround due in May/June 2025, bringing it forward to April 3–20 (a 3-week turnaround) would: (a) reduce crude demand during the acute shortage phase, (b) free that refinery's crude allocation for other units, and (c) return the refinery to full capacity by late April/early May.
- **This is the most efficient option IF the turnaround can be resourced** — turnaround contractors, spare parts, and specialist inspection teams must be available. In a crisis with multiple simultaneous operational challenges, maintenance resource competition is the binding constraint.
- **Recommendation:** PPAC should query each refinery CMD within 24 hours on turnaround schedule status. Any refinery with a turnaround due within 90 days should present a bring-forward option for crisis cell evaluation by April 2.

**DECISION IMPLICATION:** Turnaround deferral of 60–90 days is the most likely crisis tool available for 1–2 refineries in this group — PPAC must identify which refineries are in the deferral window within 24 hours, as this could free 60–120 kbpd of crude allocation for redistribution to priority nodes.
