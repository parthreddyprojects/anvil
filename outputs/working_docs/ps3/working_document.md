## Q1.15 — Fuel Gas & Hydrogen Balance at Reduced Throughput (40–60% Nameplate)

**BOTTOM LINE: At 40–50% nameplate throughput, low-complexity refineries face a fuel gas shortfall because fired heater demand does not scale linearly with crude throughput — external LPG or natural gas supplementation will be required, adding $200,000–500,000/day per refinery in fuel costs at minimum run-rate.**

**Fuel gas balance fundamentals [EXTRAPOLATED from refinery engineering principles]:**
- Fuel gas at refineries is generated from: CDU/VDU overhead gases, stabilizer off-gases, and hydroprocessing purge gases
- Fuel gas generation scales approximately linearly with throughput (~80–90% correlation)
- Fired heater fuel consumption has a significant fixed component (~30–40% of full-rate fuel demand remains constant regardless of throughput) — maintaining furnace skin temperatures, steam generation, trace heating
- **At 50% throughput:** Fuel gas generation ~50% of normal; fired heater fixed demand ~35–40% of normal total → **net shortfall of approximately 15–20% of normal fuel gas requirement**
  - Source: EXTRAPOLATED from API RP 556 (fired heater design standards); Turner & Townsend refinery energy benchmarks | **Confidence: MEDIUM**

**Hydrogen balance [EXTRAPOLATED]:**
- Hydrogen at low-complexity refineries is primarily generated by catalytic reformers (naphtha reforming)
- At 50% throughput, reformer feed is halved → hydrogen production halved
- Hydrotreater hydrogen demand does not scale exactly linearly (depends on crude sulphur content — if switching to lower-sulphur non-Hormuz crude, H₂ demand may actually decrease)
- **Net assessment:** Hydrogen balance is likely manageable at 50% throughput if switching to lower-sulphur ESPO/Bonny Light; becomes stressed if processing high-sulphur alternative crudes at reduced reformer output

## 3.11 — Equipment Degradation Risk at 40–50% Throughput for 30+ Days

**BOTTOM LINE: Sustained 40–50% throughput for 30 days carries manageable but real equipment risk at all 6 refineries; the highest-risk units are fired heaters (tube coking risk), crude preheat trains (fouling acceleration), and hydrotreater reactors (maldistribution). HPCL Mumbai's aging infrastructure and CPCL Manali's vintage units are highest risk.**

**[EXTRAPOLATED — no unit-specific inspection data in research. Based on process engineering principles and OISD guidelines.]**

**Fired heater risk (applicable to all refineries):**
- At 40–50% throughput, tube-side velocity in crude preheat furnaces falls below design minimum. Below ~40% design flow, **film boiling and local hot spots** can develop, accelerating tube coking and creep. Risk becomes significant after **15–20 days** of continuous low-rate operation.
- OISD Guideline 160 (Fired Heaters) specifies minimum flow requirements; operators must increase recirculation to maintain tube velocity. This is a standard operational response but requires continuous monitoring.
- Source: OISD-GL-160; API 560 (Fired Heaters for General Refinery Service). **Confidence: HIGH (technical standard basis).**

**HPCL Mumbai — specific risk:**
- HPCL Mumbai's refinery includes units commissioned in the 1950s–1970s (original Mahul refinery). Older tube metallurgy (carbon steel vs. modern Cr-Mo alloy) has lower tolerance for thermal cycling at reduced flow rates.
- **Assessment:** HPCL Mumbai has the highest irreversible damage risk in the group at sustained 40% throughput. Operations team should target 50–55% minimum and increase fired heater inspection frequency to daily.
- Source: HPCL Annual Report FY2024 (capex on refinery modernization ongoing, implying legacy units still in service); industry benchmark for aging CDU infrastructure. **Confidence: MEDIUM (EXTRAPOLATED).**

**CPCL Manali — specific risk:**
- Original CDU commissioned 1965 (revamped 1990s and 2000s). Crude preheat train fouling at low throughput is a documented operational challenge for high-TAN (Total Acid Number) crude processing at Manali.
- At 40% throughput with any high-TAN alternative crude (some West African grades per C04 research), **naphthenic acid corrosion risk increases** due to reduced dilution and lower flow velocity in atmospheric column overheads.
- **Assessment:** CPCL should not process high-TAN crude grades (TAN >0.5) at below 55% throughput without metallurgical inspection clearance. Limits choice of alternative crude grades.
- Source: CPCL FY2024 Annual Report (refinery configuration); Naphthenic acid corrosion industry literature (NACE SP0403). **Confidence: MEDIUM (EXTRAPOLATED).**

**NRL Numaligarh — specific risk:**
- Waxy Assam crude (pour point ~38°C, C12 research) creates wax deposition risk in cold spots during low-throughput operation. At 40–45% throughput, crude column bottom temperature may drop below wax deposition threshold in poorly insulated sections.
- **Restart after wax-plugged lines is extremely costly** — requires steam purging and potentially physical pigging of crude lines. NRL's isolation in Northeast makes emergency maintenance response slow.
- **Assessment:** NRL's minimum safe throughput floor should be set at 50% nameplate (30 kbpd), not 40%. Operating below this for >10 days risks irreversible wax deposition in CDU feed lines.
- Source: C12 (Assam crude physical properties — high pour point confirmed HIGH confidence). **Confidence: MEDIUM (EXTRAPOLATED).**

**Hydrotreater/catalyst risk (all refineries):**
- HDS and naphtha hydrotreater units have **minimum hydrogen partial pressure requirements**. At 40–50% throughput, recycle gas compressor turndown may reduce hydrogen partial pressure below catalyst design minimum, accelerating coke deposition on catalyst.
- At 30 days of sub-optimal hydrogen partial pressure, catalyst life shortens by estimated **20–40%** (requiring earlier scheduled regeneration). This is recoverable but adds planned maintenance cost post-crisis.
- Source: HALDOR TOPSOE catalyst operating guidelines (industry standard reference); API 941 (Nelson Curves for hydrogen service). **Confidence: MEDIUM (EXTRAPOLATED).**

**DECISION IMPLICATION:** Crisis cell must require each refinery's technical director to file a 48-hour equipment risk assessment specifically for CDU fired heaters and hydrotreater hydrogen partial pressure — these are the two failure modes that could convert a managed reduced-rate operation into an unplanned emergency shutdown.

**Legal exposure analysis [EXTRAPOLATED — based on OFAC enforcement framework]:**

- **OFAC's "facilitation" standard (EO 14024, Section 1(a)(iii)):** A non-US person who "facilitates" a transaction that would be prohibited if conducted by a US person may face secondary sanctions designation
- **Origin misrepresentation:** If an Indian PSU refinery **knowingly** purchases Russian crude re-invoiced as UAE origin, this creates potential exposure under OFAC's anti-evasion provisions and potentially under the **Customs Act** (India) and **FEMA** regulations

- **UAE compliance posture:** UAE has significantly increased sanctions compliance since 2023 (FATF grey-listing removal; US pressure). UAE banks and trading companies are increasingly declining to facilitate obvious Russia-origin re-invoicing

## L1.1 — Force Majeure Provisions in Crude Purchase Contracts

**EXTRAPOLATED — FM Clause Structure (reasoning chain):**
Standard crude oil term contracts (the industry norm for NOC-to-NOC or NOC-to-refiner agreements) follow Platts/ISDA-adjacent frameworks. FM clauses in these contracts **typically cover supply-side events** (force majeure on the seller's ability to deliver) rather than buyer-side payment incapacity. Key structural points:

- **Saudi Aramco term contracts:** Historically contain FM provisions triggered by events beyond the seller's control (war, natural disaster, government action). A buyer invoking FM due to *inability to pay* — as distinct from inability to lift crude — is **not standard FM territory**. FM is a supply-side instrument, not a credit instrument.
- **EXTRAPOLATED confidence: MEDIUM** — based on standard industry contract architecture; actual Aramco/ADNOC/SOMO contract language is not public.
- **Critical distinction:** The current crisis is a *logistics/sanctions/financial* crisis, not a physical force majeure. Invoking FM for non-payment would be legally aggressive and likely contested by counterparty NOCs.
- **SOMO (Iraq):** SOMO contracts historically contain relatively protective language for buyers, with dispute resolution through arbitration. Iraq's own fiscal dependence on oil revenues means SOMO would resist payment suspension.
- **KPC:** Similar structure; Kuwait has historically been a stable counterparty.

**Most protective FM scenario (EXTRAPOLATED):**
If the Hormuz mining event itself constitutes a force majeure (i.e., physical impossibility of lifting cargo through a mined strait), **seller-side FM may be invoked by Aramco/ADNOC/SOMO first**, releasing both parties from delivery/payment obligations for the duration. This is the **legally cleanest outcome** for Indian refiners — it avoids buyer-side FM invocation entirely.

- **EXTRAPOLATED confidence: MEDIUM**
- **Reasoning:** 1987-88 Gulf War tanker crisis established precedent for seller-side FM invocation during Hormuz disruption.

**MoPNG legal counsel status:** DATA GAP — no evidence in research that MoPNG has reviewed FM language across these contracts in the current scenario. **This review must be initiated within 12 hours of this briefing.**
