## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY (READ FIRST)

**The binary framing of "waiver or no waiver" is legally incorrect and strategically dangerous.** India does not hold a formal OFAC waiver for Russian crude purchases. Indian PSU refiners operate under secondary sanctions risk architecture (CAATSA Section 228, EO 14024), not primary OFAC jurisdiction. The real decision is not "extend or expire" — it is **"at what point does US enforcement posture shift from forbearance to active secondary sanctions pressure, and are Indian entities and banks positioned to absorb that shift."** Every downstream question in this bucket flows from this reframing.

**Bottom line up front:** Leadership should not structure the next 72 hours around a binary waiver extension vote. They should structure it around three parallel tracks: (1) diplomatic engagement to sustain US non-enforcement posture; (2) banking/payment channel hardening to reduce SWIFT-dependent exposure; (3) refinery-level crude inventory buffering to buy time. The fallback is not a cliff — it is a gradient of escalating risk.

## 5.11 — Third-Country Transshipment: UAE/Turkish Re-invoicing Mechanism

**ANSWER: Mechanism exists and is operationally active in the market; it carries meaningful sanctions exposure and should not be relied upon as primary fallback without formal legal opinion.**

**[EXTRAPOLATED] Mechanism Description:**
Structure: Rosneft/Russian seller → UAE trading company (e.g., Titan Oil, Coral Energy) → re-invoiced as "UAE Blend" or "Middle East origin" → sold to Indian PSU refiner

This structure has been reported as active in 2022–2024 (Reuters, Bloomberg multiple reports on "shadow fleet" and origin-blending practices | **Confidence: MEDIUM** on existence of market practice).

**Legal exposure analysis [EXTRAPOLATED — based on OFAC enforcement framework]:**

- **OFAC's "facilitation" standard (EO 14024, Section 1(a)(iii)):** A non-US person who "facilitates" a transaction that would be prohibited if conducted by a US person may face secondary sanctions designation
- **Origin misrepresentation:** If an Indian PSU refinery **knowingly** purchases Russian crude re-invoiced as UAE origin, this creates potential exposure under OFAC's anti-evasion provisions and potentially under the **Customs Act** (India) and **FEMA** regulations
- **"Knowingly" standard:** This is the critical variable. If the Indian refiner genuinely cannot determine origin (crude quality testing cannot always distinguish ESPO from other light crudes), legal exposure is reduced. If the refiner is informed of the actual origin by the intermediary, exposure is real.
- **UAE compliance posture:** UAE has significantly increased sanctions compliance since 2023 (FATF grey-listing removal; US pressure). UAE banks and trading companies are increasingly declining to facilitate obvious Russia-origin re-invoicing
  - Source: Reuters, multiple 2023–2024 reports on UAE compliance tightening | **Confidence: HIGH on trend; MEDIUM on current state**

**Assessment:** This mechanism is **not a viable primary fallback** for PSU refiners. It:
1. Requires PSU refiners (state-owned entities with government directors) to maintain plausible deniability that is likely unsustainable
2. Exposes Indian entities to US "facilitation" sanctions
3. UAE trading counterparty risk is increasing not decreasing
4. Has no Cabinet-level authorization and cannot receive one (authorizing origin misrepresentation is legally impossible for GOI)

**DECISION IMPLICATION:** MoPNG must explicitly direct PSU refinery management that re-invoiced Russian crude through UAE/Turkish intermediaries without proper legal vetting is not an authorized fallback mechanism, and any such arrangement requires formal sanctions counsel clearance before execution.
