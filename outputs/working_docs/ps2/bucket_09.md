## 9.2 — Financial Commitment Authority: Chairman vs. Board, and Emergency Authorization Mechanisms

**DATA GAP (specific IOCL thresholds) + EXTRAPOLATED from Companies Act 2013 + DPE Maharatna Guidelines | Confidence: MEDIUM**

**Bottom line: The Chairman can likely authorize single crude purchase transactions up to ₹1,000–2,000 crore unilaterally under standard Board delegation. At $156/bbl, a single VLCC cargo costs ₹900–1,100 crore. A 5-cargo emergency procurement package costs ₹4,500–5,500 crore — requiring Board authorization. A circular resolution can achieve this within 6–12 hours if Company Secretary is activated now.**

### Statutory Framework
- **Companies Act 2013, Section 179**: Board of Directors has inherent powers; delegation to Chairman/MD is permissible but bounded by Board resolution specifying limits.
- **DPE Guidelines for Maharatna CPSEs (2010, updated 2021)**: Maharatna companies have the highest autonomy — Board can delegate to Chairman/CMD up to **₹5,000 crore per project** for investment decisions. However, **crude purchase contracts** are treated as operational expenditure, not capital investment, and sit under separate operating authority delegations.
- **Typical CPSE operating delegation (extrapolated from DPE norms)**: Chairman can approve individual procurement contracts up to ~**₹500–1,000 crore** without Board approval; above this, Board/Committee of Directors authorization is required.
- **DATA GAP**: IOCL's specific Board-approved delegation matrix is not in the public domain. The Company Secretary holds this document. **The Chairman must instruct the Company Secretary to extract and circulate the exact thresholds within 2 hours.**

### Emergency Authorization Mechanisms (Confirmed Statutory Options)

| Mechanism | Authority | Timeline | Legal Validity |
|-----------|-----------|----------|---------------|
| **Circular Resolution** | Companies Act 2013, S.175 | 6–12 hours if directors are available | Valid for most Board decisions except items requiring ordinary/special resolution at GM |
| **Committee of Directors** (if Board has constituted one with delegated powers) | Board resolution constituting committee | Immediate if committee exists | Valid within committee's delegated mandate |
| **Emergency EGM** | Companies Act 2013, S.100 | Minimum 21 days notice (or shorter with 95% shareholder consent) — **impractical for 72-hour window** | Valid but not usable here |
| **Ratification post-facto** | Companies Act 2013, S.180 | Chairman acts; Board ratifies within 3 months | Legally risky; use only as last resort |

### Most Viable Path: Circular Resolution
- **Mechanics**: Company Secretary circulates resolution draft via email/WhatsApp to all Board members; directors indicate approval in writing (email or digital signature); resolution passes on receiving majority approval; no physical meeting required.
- **Timeline**: If Company Secretary is activated at 0600 hrs April 3, a circular resolution approving an emergency crude procurement package of up to ₹6,000 crore can be passed by **1800 hrs April 3**.
- **Precedent**: CPSEs including ONGC and IOCL have used circular resolutions for time-sensitive approvals during COVID-19 supply disruptions (2020–21).
- **Confidence: MEDIUM** — precedent is confirmed; IOCL-specific use in crude procurement context is extrapolated.

### Specific Cost Reference (Scenario-Grounded)
- At $156/bbl, a VLCC cargo (2 million barrels) costs approximately **₹1,040 crore** at ₹84/USD exchange rate
- Emergency 90-day procurement package (estimated 15 VLCCs) = **₹15,600 crore total commitment**
- This **unambiguously requires Board authorization** — Chairman cannot commit to this unilaterally
- Practical approach: Board-authorize a **framework resolution** granting Chairman authority to execute crude purchases up to ₹15,000 crore over 90 days within defined parameters, rather than individual transaction approvals

**DECISION IMPLICATION:** The Company Secretary must be activated immediately to run a circular resolution by 1800 hrs April 3 authorizing a framework crude procurement mandate — failure to do so means every cargo above ~₹1,000 crore faces legal authorization risk.

## 9.3 — MoPNG Crisis Coordinator: Authority, Legal Instrument, and 24/7 Directive Power

**DATA GAP (no designated crisis coordinator confirmed) + EXTRAPOLATED from ESMA 1968, Petroleum Act 1934, ECA 1955 | Confidence: MEDIUM**

**Bottom line: No standing MoPNG Crisis Coordinator with 24/7 directive authority exists in confirmed public documentation. The Secretary (Petroleum) holds inherent administrative authority but without a fresh notification, inter-ministerial consultation requirements will slow directives to HPCL, BPCL, ONGC by 12–48 hours. A fresh ESMA notification or ECA order can be issued within 24 hours and should be prepared now.**

### What Exists (Confirmed)
- **ESMA 1968 (Central)**: Covers LPG supply as an essential service. **Invocation prevents worker strikes/stoppages but does NOT mandate refinery production levels or crude sourcing decisions.** Source: Essential Services Maintenance Act 1968, verified March 2025. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Essential Commodities Act (ECA) 1955, amended 2020**: Government can direct OMCs to maintain buffer stocks, prioritize PDS LPG supply, and control distribution. Criminal penalties up to 7 years for hoarding. Source: ECA 1955. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- **Petroleum Act 1934 + Petroleum and Natural Gas Rules 1959**: MoPNG has regulatory authority over refinery operations, crude import licensing, and product distribution. Secretary (Petroleum) can issue administrative directions to PSU refiners without ministerial approval for operational matters.
- **CCEA (Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs)**: Required for decisions with fiscal impact >₹500 crore (standard threshold) or pricing decisions. This is the correct forum for price revision, oil bond reissuance, or DBT authorization.

### EXTRAPOLATED: What MoPNG Secretary Can Do Unilaterally
1. **Issue written operational directives** to IOCL, HPCL, BPCL, ONGC as their administrative ministry — no fresh notification required for directions on crude sourcing priorities, run-rate guidance, or inventory reporting
2. **Invoke ESMA** (with Home Ministry coordination) within 24 hours — protects LPG supply chain workforce from strike action; requires gazette notification
3. **Issue ECA control order** on LPG/HSD distribution priorities within 24 hours — requires inter-ministerial concurrence with MoF (fast-track possible)
4. **Cannot unilaterally**: revise retail fuel prices (political/CCEA level); commit DBT payments >₹500 crore (MoF/CCEA required); authorize G2G crude contracts (MEA/Cabinet involvement needed)

### Recommended Governance Action
- **Designate Joint Secretary (Refinery)** as 24/7 Crisis Coordinator with explicit written authority from Secretary (Petroleum) to issue same-day directives to all PSU refiners
- Establish **dedicated crisis hotline** linking MoPNG, IOCL Chairman's office, HMEL MD's office, and PPAC
- Draft ESMA notification and ECA control order **now** for Secretary's signature — these are 4-hour drafting exercises, not 4-day ones
- First directive to issue: **mandatory daily situation report** from IOCL/HMEL/HPCL/BPCL by 0600 hrs daily (see Q9.4)

**DECISION IMPLICATION:** MoPNG Secretary must issue a written designation of crisis coordinator and standing directive authority by April 3, 1200 hrs — every hour of ambiguity on directive authority translates directly into slower OMC response times.

## 9.5 — HMEL JV Governance: MD's Emergency Authority vs. Mittal Energy Consent Requirements

**DATA GAP (JV agreement not public) + EXTRAPOLATED from Companies Act 2013 + standard JV governance norms + HMEL ownership structure | Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM**

**Bottom line: HMEL's MD almost certainly cannot make material run-rate or sourcing changes unilaterally — Mittal Energy's 48.99% stake and near-equal board representation gives it effective veto power over operational decisions above a materiality threshold. This is the single most structurally complex governance problem in the crisis. HPCL must invoke whatever emergency authority provisions exist in the SHA within 24 hours, or face paralysis on HMEL decisions.**

### Ownership and Governance Structure (Confirmed)
- **HPCL**: 48.99% equity | **Mittal Energy Investments (Lakshmi Mittal)**: 48.99% | Others: ~2%
- Source: HMEL AR FY24. **Confidence: HIGH.**
- At near-equal shareholding, **neither party has unilateral control**. Standard JV governance under a Shareholders' Agreement (SHA) and Articles of Association would require consent of both major shareholders for "reserved matters" — which typically include: changes in run-rate >10–15% of nameplate; crude procurement contracts above a materiality threshold; changes to crude slate strategy; and capital expenditure above agreed limits.

### EXTRAPOLATED: What MD Can and Cannot Do

**Likely within MD's day-to-day authority** (standard CPSE/JV operational delegation):
- Run-rate adjustments within ±5–8% without board approval
- Crude grade substitutions within existing approved slate parameters
- Activation of pre-approved alternative suppliers within contracted frameworks
- Emergency maintenance shutdowns (safety-driven, unilateral authority)

**Likely requiring Mittal Energy consent** (based on standard JV SHA provisions):
- Run-rate reduction >15% (from 173 kbpd to <147 kbpd) — this represents a material change in commercial output
- New crude supply contracts above ~$200–300 million (extrapolated materiality threshold)
- Changes to crude sourcing strategy (e.g., moving away from Russian Urals, which is 55% of slate)
- Any decision to draw down strategic reserves beyond normal operating parameters

### Critical Complication: Russian Crude Dependency
- HMEL's crude slate is **55% Russian Urals/ESPO** (HMEL Q4FY24, PPAC; **Confidence: MEDIUM**)
- If the Hormuz crisis scenario also disrupts Russian crude routing or if sanctions concerns arise, **any alternative sourcing decision requires Mittal Energy's board-level consent**
- Mittal Energy is a commercial entity with profit-maximizing incentives — its consent cannot be assumed in a high-cost crude environment

### EXTRAPOLATED: HPCL Emergency Override Options
1. **SHA Emergency Provisions**: Most JV agreements between government and private entities include emergency authority clauses allowing the government partner to act unilaterally during a declared national emergency. HPCL's legal team must confirm within 4 hours whether such a clause exists in HMEL's SHA. **Confidence: MEDIUM** (standard drafting practice, not confirmed for HMEL specifically).
2. **Government Direction Under Petroleum Act**: MoPNG can issue a direction to HMEL as a "petroleum undertaking" under Petroleum Act 1934 — this is a statutory direction that overrides commercial JV governance. This has never been tested in a JV context but is theoretically available.
3. **Board Resolution Through HPCL-Appointed Directors**: HPCL appoints a proportional share of HMEL's Board. HPCL-appointed directors can call an emergency board meeting (shorter notice for urgent matters under Companies Act) and attempt to pass resolutions — but Mittal Energy's directors can block if SHA gives them veto rights.
4. **Direct Engagement with Mittal Energy at Principal Level**: The most pragmatic path. HPCL CMD or MoPNG Secretary engaging Lakshmi Mittal's office directly to secure voluntary cooperation, framed as national emergency, with commercial compensation commitments. This is faster than legal mechanisms.

### Key Risk
**If Mittal Energy withholds consent and no statutory override is successfully invoked, HMEL (180 kbpd, NCI 12.0) — India's most complex inland refinery — effectively cannot be directed to change operations during the crisis.** This represents a catastrophic governance gap.

**DECISION IMPLICATION:** HPCL CMD must personally engage Mittal Energy's Principal Representative within 6 hours to secure voluntary operational cooperation under a written emergency framework — simultaneously, HMEL's legal team must confirm SHA emergency clause existence by April 4, 0800 hrs.
