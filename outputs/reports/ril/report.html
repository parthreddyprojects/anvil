Reliance Industries (Jamnagar): Crisis Brief

Decision Deadline: April 3, 2026 | Indian Basket: $156/bbl
Problem Statement

Reliance Industries Jamnagar (1.4 mbpd) faces a simultaneous triple-threat by April 3: expiry of US sanctions forbearance on Russian crude (~35–40% of crude diet, ~490–560 kbd), Hormuz mining suspending normal Middle East liftings, and working capital doubling to ~$312M per VLCC at $156/bbl Indian basket. Without secured replacement crude and confirmed credit headroom within 72 hours, Jamnagar faces a forced throughput cut to ~1.1 mbpd within 15–18 days and forfeits the $4–6/bbl GRM uplift window created by widening crack spreads and the MRPL supply vacuum. The entire capitalize-and-capture posture is sequentially dependent on one prior condition: crude supply continuity must be confirmed before run-rate maximization or balance-sheet deployment is authorized.

Decision Sensitivity: The capitalize-versus-protect inflection point sits at $142/bbl Indian basket: above $142, crack spreads on diesel, jet, and naphtha remain wide enough that aggressive run maximization, spot crude procurement at premium, and petrochemical feedstock locking generate positive incremental margin even a

Core Hypotheses

H1
Jamnagar can generate $3–6M/day of incremental gross refining margin uplift within 24 hours by pushing yield optimization and run-rate to nameplate capacity (1.4 mbpd), without any new crude procurement, by maximizing vacuum unit throughput and coker yield on existing inventory — delivering $270–540M of incremental contribution across Q2 FY2026 at zero capital cost.
Break point: Indian basket falls below $142/bbl, at which point elevated crude inventory cost exceeds incremental product revenue and the run-rate maximization becomes margin-negative; or if crude inventory cover drops below 12 days before replacement cargoes arrive, forcing a rate cut that makes the yield optimization moot. | Status: UNCERTAIN (MEDIUM)
H2
Omani crude (PDO/DME Oman Blend, 33° API, 1.0% sulfur) is the only immediately actionable Hormuz-independent, chemically compatible replacement for Russian Urals at Jamnagar, and RIL's Head of Supply must fix a minimum of 4–6 Oman spot cargoes (8–12 million barrels) within 6 hours to secure April–May supply continuity — at an estimated landed premium of $2–4/bbl over prior Urals cost but $8–12/bbl below the cost of defaulting to West African light-sweet on a full-diet basis.
Break point: If Omani spot availability is exhausted by competing Asian buyers (Korea, Japan, China face identical procurement crises) before RIL fixtures are confirmed, or if the DME Oman spot premium exceeds $6/bbl over Urals equivalent, the economics shift to a blended West Africa/Arab Light strategy and the transit-time gap requires an immediate throughput rate cut of 15–20% to prevent inventory depletion. | Status: UNCERTAIN (LOW)
H3
RIL's CEO must execute a direct call to Saudi Aramco within 12 hours to secure Arab Light via Yanbu (Red Sea loading, Hormuz-bypass) at an estimated $3–5/bbl premium over normal OSP, targeting 2–4 additional cargoes (4–8 mb) for April–May delivery — this is the only proven high-volume Hormuz-independent Middle East supply route, and Aramco will preferentially allocate Yanbu capacity to strategic long-term customers who engage at CEO level before the spot market prices in the crisis premium.
Break point: If Saudi Aramco's East-West Pipeline is already running at or above 4 mb/d utilization (capacity ceiling) due to emergency diversion of its own domestic volumes, no incremental Yanbu allocation is available to RIL regardless of relationship or pricing, and the entire Arab Light substitution strategy is foreclosed; also flips if the OSP premium exceeds $7/bbl, at which point the $142/bbl basket economics require reassessment. | Status: UNCERTAIN (LOW)
H4
RIL should immediately redirect Jamnagar SEZ product exports of diesel and ATF to the domestic southern India market vacated by MRPL's shutdown, capturing a $4–7/bbl domestic spot premium above Singapore export parity on an estimated 80–100 kbd of incremental supply — generating $320–700K/day of additional margin versus export while simultaneously creating a regulatory goodwill asset with GoI worth more than its direct financial value in the current crisis.
Break point: If MoPNG formally invokes Essential Commodities Act direction to supply domestic market (converting a voluntary premium-capture decision into a mandated obligation at regulated prices), the $4–7/bbl premium collapses to a regulated margin and the financial case disappears; also flips if Singapore gasoil crack spreads widen more than 30% above current levels, making export economics superior to domestic diversion. | Status: UNCERTAIN (LOW)
H5
RIL's CFO must draw maximum available revolving credit within 24 hours — estimated $3–5B incremental draw — to fund the surge in crude working capital requirements at $156/bbl (each VLCC cargo now costs $312M versus $160M at the $80/bbl 2024 average), pre-empting any bank covenant review or correspondent bank pullback triggered by the April 3 sanctions deadline, and securing the financial runway to execute 6–10 spot crude cargoes simultaneously without triggering cross-default clauses.
Break point: If RIL's revolving credit facilities contain Material Adverse Change (MAC) clauses that are triggered by the April 3 OFAC enforcement posture change — specifically if any facility documentation references sanctions-related MAC events — drawing down on those facilities could itself constitute an event of default; the break point is the legal opinion confirming no MAC trigger exists, without which the draw-down strategy is operationally correct but legally dangerous. | Status: UNCERTAIN (MEDIUM)
H6
RIL should lock petrochemical feedstock (naphtha and LPG) at current spot prices for 60–90 days within 48 hours, capturing an estimated $2–4/bbl feedstock cost advantage relative to the projected forward price of naphtha in a sustained $156/bbl crude environment — but only if live cracker utilization confirms the complex is running above 80%, indicating market demand is sufficient to absorb output; if utilization is below 80%, RIL should redirect refinery naphtha to export markets at crisis premiums instead.
Break point: If Jamnagar cracker utilization is confirmed below 80% (indicating product-market constraint from weak Chinese petrochemical demand as flagged in Bucket 1, Q1.13), the feedstock locking recommendation inverts entirely — locking naphtha supply at high prices into a cracker running at sub-economic margins increases cash losses per ton of output; the threshold is 80% cracker utilization as the minimum for the feedstock lock to be NPV-positive. | Status: UNCERTAIN (MEDIUM)
H7
RIL must engage MoPNG at Secretary-level within 24 hours with a proactive MRPL gap-fill supply plan — committing to supply 80–100 kbd of diesel and ATF to Karnataka/Goa via coastal tanker — and convert this national emergency obligation into a negotiated package that includes: GoI-backed emergency LC guarantees for spot crude procurement, customs duty waivers on non-Russian replacement crude, and a formal written clarification from MEA/MoPNG on post-April 3 US sanctions enforcement posture; the value of this regulatory package exceeds $200–400M in direct financial support across Q2 FY2026.
Break point: If GoI invokes Essential Commodities Act mandatory supply direction before RIL initiates the voluntary engagement, the leverage evaporates — RIL becomes an obligated supplier at regulated margins with no quid pro quo; the break point is the 48-hour window before MoPNG's own crisis response protocols force a mandatory allocation order. | Status: UNCERTAIN (MEDIUM)
H8
If Russian crude loss exceeds 60% of current Jamnagar volumes post-April 3 (approximately 490–560 kbd of the estimated 770–840 kbd currently sourced from Russia), RIL must immediately flip to a defensive posture — cut throughput to 1.1–1.2 mbpd (78–86% utilization), shorten crude procurement tenors to 30 days maximum, increase product hedging to 50% of Q2 output, and defer the balance-sheet deployment recommended in H5 — because above this threshold, crude cost disadvantage structurally exceeds the crack spread opportunity regardless of basket price.
Break point: The 60% Russian volume loss threshold is the secondary decision sensitivity explicitly stated in the problem statement — at exactly 60% loss, the crude cost disadvantage equals the crack spread opportunity; above 60%, every additional percentage point of Russian volume lost generates net margin destruction even at $156/bbl basket, making the defensive posture the only rational response. | Status: CONFIRMED (MEDIUM)

Hypothesis Graveyard

KILLED: Jamnagar SEZ production can be redirected to the domestic southern India market at $4–7/bbl premium capturing 80–100 kbd of incremental domestic supply — The SEZ Act mandates 100% export from SEZ units; domestic supply requires DTA clearance with customs duty payment that eliminates the premium. The SEZ unit (208 kbd) is legally unavailable for domestic diversion without regulatory approval that cannot clear in 72 hours. The logistics cap of 30–50 kbd from DTA and coastal tanker constraints limits the entire domestic diversion opportunity to 20–35 kbd, destroying the scale assumption of the hypothesis. The '80–100 kbd' figure is implausible by a factor of 2.5–4x.
KILLED: Yield optimization and nameplate run-rate generate $270–540M of incremental contribution across Q2 FY2026 — The Q2 contribution projection is mathematically inconsistent with the working capital constraint identified in the same document. At $156/bbl, Jamnagar at nameplate (1.4 mbpd) consumes approximately $218M/day in crude costs. The document identifies residual revolving credit headroom of $2–3B — sufficient for roughly 9–14 days of incremental working capital above current utilization, not 90 days of Q2 operation. The Q2 projection requires the credit draw in H5 to be confirmed first; H5 is rated UNCERTAIN; therefore the Q2 projection cannot be HIGH confidence. The $3–6M/day figure may hold for the first 9–14 days; the extrapolation to $270–540M across Q2 is unsupported.

If nothing is done by April 3, Jamnagar exhausts current crude inventory in 18–25 days, is forced to cut throughput 15–20%, and surrenders the MRPL supply vacuum arbitrage window to IOC, HPCL, and BPCL coastal dispatches.

CEO Review

REVISE (Clarity: 7/10 | Actionability: 8/10 | Credibility: 6/10)

The analytical work here is genuinely strong — the team caught the internal contradictions in the hypothesis tree, correctly sequenced crude confirmation before run-rate authorization, and the MAC clause flag is exactly the kind of thing that should be in a crisis brief. But I can't sign Decision 2 without a sanctions counsel opinion already in motion, I can't sign Decision 1 without knowing whether Oman spot even has cargo availability given market-wide Hormuz stress, and I can't validate the entire risk framing until someone tells me whether the Hormuz mining claim is confirmed intelligence or a scenario assumption. The brief is hiding two things behind confident language: the inventory number is a range when it should be a fact, and the Hormuz risk is presented as near-certain when it may be speculative. Fix those, get me a preliminary legal read on MAC exposure, and confirm Oman spot availability — then bring this back. I'm not rejecting the direction, I'm rejecting the gaps.

Problems
  • The Hormuz mining claim is unverified and load-bearing. '~700–900 kbd of Middle East term liftings suspended' with 'force majeure notices expected within days' — expected by whom? If this is speculative, the entire triple-threat framing collapses to a single sanctions risk, and the urgency calculus changes significantly.
  • The 60–70% crude supply at-risk figure in the analysis directly contradicts the confidence of the recommendation header. If 60–70% of crude diet is simultaneously at risk, this isn't a 24-hour sprint to capitalize — it's a potential existential operational crisis. The brief doesn't reconcile this tension; it buries it in the analysis section.
  • The Q2 $270–540M incremental contribution is never fully derived in this document. The $3–6M/day GRM figure times 90 days is $270–540M — fine, but that assumes nameplate throughput for the full quarter with confirmed replacement crude and no credit constraint. All three of those conditions are currently unmet. This number is being used to justify aggressive action but is built on unconfirmed assumptions.
  • MAC clause risk is flagged but not sized. If Western revolving credit facilities freeze due to OFAC enforcement posture shift, what is the actual credit gap? The brief says $2–3B residual headroom 'may' be legally dangerous but doesn't tell me the fallback if counsel says the draw is blocked. Decision 2 is structurally incomplete without that scenario.
  • The Aramco CEO direct call as a 12-hour ask is unrealistic without knowing current relationship status, existing term contract obligations, and whether Yanbu loading slots are physically available. This reads like a wish, not an action.
  • The domestic arbitrage numbers are internally contradicted. The brief corrects the hypothesis tree's $320–700K/day to $80–245K/day, which is good intellectual honesty — but then Decision 3 treats MoPNG engagement as a high-value strategic lever. If the direct margin is $80–245K/day on 20–35 kbd, the leverage argument has to carry the entire weight of that decision. The brief asserts diplomatic value 'materially exceeds direct margin' without quantifying it at all.
  • Inventory cover range is too wide. '18–25 days estimated' — that's a 7-day spread on the most critical number in this brief. Is this an estimate or is there a live tank measurement? The difference between 18 and 25 days changes the decision timeline materially.
  • No mention of shipping availability or VLCC fixture lead times for West African/USGC alternatives. If the procurement-replacement lead time is 30 days, I need to know whether VLCCs are available to load in time, at what freight premium, and whether the $5/bbl landed premium threshold already accounts for elevated freight. It probably doesn't.
Missing
  • Source and confidence level for the Hormuz mining / force majeure claim — is this confirmed intelligence, market rumor, or internal risk modeling?
  • Current confirmed crude inventory in absolute barrels, not a day-range estimate. I need the actual number.
  • Existing Aramco term contract status — volume, pricing formula, remaining Q2 obligations — before I authorize a CEO-to-CEO call requesting spot cargoes that may already be contractually owed.
  • Legal opinion or at minimum a preliminary verbal from sanctions counsel on MAC clause exposure — this should have been initiated before this brief landed on my desk.
  • VLCC availability and freight cost for West African/USGC routes factored into the $5/bbl premium threshold.
  • Fallback plan if Decision 2 counsel opinion is negative — i.e., Western credit draw is legally blocked. What is the non-Western credit capacity alone, and does it cover the cargo requirement?
  • Confirmation that Oman DME spot market has 4–6 cargo availability at any price right now, given every other refiner in the region is presumably facing the same Hormuz exposure.
  • Timeline for DTA legal clearance — the brief says it 'cannot clear in 72 hours' but gives no actual timeline, which makes Decision 3's 24-hour MoPNG engagement feel disconnected from legal reality.
