## 1. OVERALL IMPRESSION

**I would not send this to a client CEO in its current form.** But it's closer than 90% of what I see.

Here's what's right: The storyline is genuinely compelling. The scenario framework is solid. The data density is appropriate for the gravity of the situation. The design system is professional — dark theme, monospace numbers, consistent color coding. This is clearly built by someone who understands both the subject matter and visual communication.

Here's what's wrong: **You built a Bloomberg terminal, not a McKinsey deck.** Every card is trying to be a comprehensive dashboard rather than making one irrefutable point. The audience — Indian energy C-suite — has 3-4 seconds per card before deciding whether to keep scrolling. You're giving them 45-second cards. That's a fatal mismatch.

The deeper structural problem: **I can only review 5 of 26 cards because you've only shown me Cards 1-5.** But those 5 cards alone contain more information than most 30-slide decks. If the remaining 21 cards maintain this density, you have a 200-slide deck compressed into 26 scroll-snap cards. That's not economy — that's compression injury.

**Verdict: 7/10 on substance, 4/10 on discipline, 5/10 on executability as a briefing format.**

## 10. THE DECK AS A WHOLE

### What I can assess from 5 of 26 cards:

**Story arc (partial view):** Act I is situation/diagnosis. The progression from "strait closed" → "escalating" → "macro damage" → "onshore impact" → "alternatives limited" is logical. It follows the classic McKinsey situation-complication-resolution structure, and you're still in complication at Card 5. Good.

**What concerns me about the remaining 21 cards:**

If Cards 6-26 maintain this density, the reader will experience cognitive fatigue by Card 8-10. **The human brain can process ~4 novel data points per minute in a scroll format.** Each of your cards contains 8-12 data points. At 26 cards, that's 200+ data points. No executive retains that.

**What the arc needs:**

1. **A clear "so what" pivot around Card 8-10.** Stop telling me the situation and start telling me what to do about it. If that exists, great. If it doesn't, the deck is a situation report, not a strategic briefing — and the title promises the latter.

2. **A decision-forcing mechanism.** The scenario framework (Card 2's probability bars) is the right tool, but it needs to connect explicitly to actions: "If prolonged standoff (35-40%), do X within 48 hours. If conflagration (30-35%), do Y immediately."

3. **A 3-item action list on the final card.** Not 10 items. Not a "checklist." Three things the CEO should authorize on Monday morning. Each with a dollar value, a deadline, and a named owner.

4. **The climax should be the cost of inaction.** "If you do nothing for 30 more days, here is the specific financial impact to your company." Not India's macro. Not the global oil market. *Your company, your P&L, your board.*

### Final structural recommendation:

**Cut the deck to 18-20 cards max.** Use the following structure:

| Section | Cards | Purpose |
|---------|-------|---------|
| Opener | 1-2 | "Here's what happened, here's why it matters to you" |
| Situation | 3-6 | Four proof points (macro, supply, alternatives, escalation) |
| Scenario Framework | 7-8 | Probabilities + price ranges + duration |
| Impact Quantification | 9-12 | Your company's specific exposure under each scenario |
| Response Options | 13-16 | What peers are doing, what's available, trade-offs |
| Action Plan | 17-18 | 3 decisions, 3 deadlines, 3 price tags |
| Appendix | 19-20 | Methodology, sources, glossary |

---

## BOTTOM LINE

**This briefing has A-grade intelligence packed into C-grade information architecture.** The data is real, the analysis is sharp, the scenario framework is genuinely useful, and the design system is polished. But you're treating every card like it might be the last one the reader sees, so you cram everything in. The paradox: by trying to ensure nothing is missed, you ensure almost everything is missed.

**The single most important edit:** Go through every card and ask — *"What is the ONE sentence a CEO would repeat to their board?"* Everything else on that card exists only to prove that sentence. If it doesn't prove it, it goes to the appendix or it dies.

This has the bones of an exceptional product. It needs an editor with a machete, not a scalpel.
